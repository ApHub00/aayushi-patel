Using AI in Market Research

A working handbook for marketing intelligence students who want to use AI the way agency and client-side researchers actually use it.

Author · Aayushi PatelAdopted across MSMI practicum cohorts7 sections + recommendations
TL;DR
  • AI is now mainstream across the entire market research workflow — from scoping a brief to writing the final deck — but it works best as a "force multiplier" for a human researcher, not a replacement. Every serious industry voice (Ipsos, Kantar, ESOMAR, NewMR) converges on the same rule: AI accelerates the grunt work, humans own the judgment, interpretation, and story.
  • The single biggest student mistake is treating AI like a search engine ("tell me about my market") and trusting the output. The second biggest is pasting confidential client or participant data into public tools. Learn prompting frameworks, grounding/fact-checking, and data hygiene before anything else.

Section 1: Using AI to Identify Key Business Issues & Refine Research Questions

The core skill: going from a vague client brief ("we're losing younger customers, find out why") to a scoped, answerable research question. AI is genuinely good at this if you make it interrogate the brief rather than answer it.

Prompting techniques that work:

  • Persona prompting: Open with "You are an expert market researcher with 30 years of experience." This is the single most common opening in practitioner prompt libraries and measurably sharpens output.
  • Chain-of-Thought (CoT): Ask the model to reason step by step before answering. Peer-reviewed work confirms CoT "significantly reduces hallucinations in prompt-sensitive scenarios." For scoping, this means asking the AI to first list what it would need to know, then propose research questions.
  • Layered prompting: Start broad, review, then narrow. The best results usually come from a back-and-forth process, not a single magic prompt.
  • The CLEAR framework (Context, Language, Expectation, Action, Reflection) from HubSpot's Market Research Kit - a clean structure students can memorize.
  • Reverse prompting : After explaining your project by voice, ask "Given all this, what do you think would be the best thing that I should even be asking you?" This surfaces blind spots.

Real example prompts (from practitioner libraries):

  • Problem identification: "Identify the main research problem based on the following project description: [insert]. Focus on any gaps in customer experience with product X that the client hasn't clearly addressed."
  • Gap surfacing from data: "[Insert product reviews] Based on the customer feedback provided: What critical gaps in product satisfaction are repeatedly mentioned but may not be well understood by stakeholders?"
  • Objective prioritization: use Porter's Five Forces or a SWOT scaffold and have AI generate a first draft you refine with internal knowledge (HubSpot).

Mapping stakeholder needs & prioritizing objectives: Use AI to convert a messy brief into a structured research plan- stakeholders, decisions each stakeholder needs to make, knowledge gaps, and objectives ranked by decision impact. The key is to ask the right questions.

Section 2: Using AI for Secondary Research

This is where AI delivers the fastest, safest wins for a student - but before understand the tools.

The critical distinction — web-search AI vs. training-data AI:

  • ChatGPT/Claude (base mode) generate from training data with a knowledge cutoff. Strength: synthesis, reasoning, strategic framing. Weakness: no live data, and a documented tendency to fabricate citations.
  • Perplexity is an "answer engine" - it searches the live web in real time and returns cited answers with links. Its architecture "fundamentally solves the hallucination problem" for factual claims because every claim is traceable to a source.
  • The pro move: use both. Perplexity for current facts and sourcing; ChatGPT/Claude for synthesis and narrative.

The specialist tools and their specific strengths:

  • Elicit (elicit.org): AI research assistant over 125M+ academic papers. Best for structured data extraction across many papers — sample sizes, methods, effect sizes in a spreadsheet view — plus systematic-review-style screening and Report mode (up to ~500 sources). Use: for academic/evidence-base building.
  • Gigabrain: AI-powered Reddit/forum search — surfaces what real users say in discussion threads. Best for authentic voice-of-customer and barrier discovery from social conversation.
  • Perplexity: Best all-round live-web secondary research with citations.

A secondary research workflow (student-ready):

  1. Scope the question (Section 1).
  2. Live-web scan with Perplexity → gather current facts + sources.
  3. Academic/evidence layer with Elicit for anything that needs rigor.
  4. Social/voice layer with Gigabrain for real customer language and pain points.
  5. Synthesize in ChatGPT/Claude → paste your sourced findings and ask for structured analysis.
  6. Fact-check every number against the original source before it enters a deliverable.

Real prompt examples:

  • Market sizing: "Estimate the market size for [luxury electric vehicles in North America]. State your methodology list every assumption, and flag which figures should be verified against Statista, IBISWorld, or government data."
  • Competitive landscape: "Compare [Competitor A/B/C] on product features, pricing, and brand perception using public sources. Then produce a SWOT for each and cite every claim."
  • Barrier identification: "Analyze these customer reviews. Identify recurring pain points, feature requests, and frustrations, grouped into usability, service, price, and trust."
  • Review extraction: "Extract top frustrations from these competitor reviews and categorize the emotional language in negative reviews."

How to avoid hallucinations (the fact-checking toolkit):

  • Grounding: give the model the source material (reviews, reports, transcripts) and instruct it to answer only from that. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is the technical version.
  • Explicit constraints: "If you are not certain, say so. Do not invent statistics or citations."
  • Chain-of-Thought: forces reasoning steps you can inspect.
  • Few-shot prompting: give one or two worked examples of the output you want.
  • Consistency checks: ask the same question multiple ways; divergence signals unreliability.
  • Citation verification: ChatGPT and Bing show critical reference-hallucination rates in studies; Elicit and SciSpace show "negligible hallucination." Always click the link.

Section 3: Using AI for Qualitative Research

Best practices (the settled consensus): AI handles transcription, first-pass coding, and pattern-spotting at superhuman speed; humans own interpretation, cultural nuance, and the "so what." Ipsos tested AI on transcription, translation, and sentiment analysis and found: "In thematic analysis, generative AI provided good topline summaries but struggled to elevate insights and draw business implications compared to human researchers. Human intervention is still necessary to bridge the gaps in domain expertise, bias identification, and hallucination prevention."

Developing topic/discussion guides — example prompts:

  • "You are an expert qualitative moderator. Draft a 60-minute discussion guide for in-depth interviews exploring why [target] switched away from [brand]. Include a warm-up, 4-5 thematic sections with probes, and a projective exercise. Objectives: [insert]."
  • "Review this discussion guide. Flag leading questions, double-barrelled questions, and gaps against my objectives."

How AI analyzes qualitative data: thematic coding (auto-suggesting codes/themes across a dataset), sentiment analysis, and pattern recognition across volumes no human could read line-by-line. The rigor caveat: interpretive steps (naming, refining, writing up themes) must stay human-led.

AI-moderated interview platforms:

  • Outset: AI-moderated research platform; video/voice/text, auto-generates guides from research goals, screen-share for usability testing, Figma integration, 40+ languages, "Abyss" mode allows up to 10 layers of follow-up probing, fraud detection tagged at 99%+ accuracy.
  • Glaut: voice-first "AI-moderated interviews (AIMIs)" combining qual depth with quant scale; trusted by Ipsos and Kantar.

Section 4: Using AI for Quantitative Research

Best practice: AI is excellent for survey building, code generation, cleaning, and exploratory analysis — but you must verify the statistics and understand the methods. It lowers the technical barrier; it does not remove the need for statistical literacy.

AI survey builders:

  • Qualtrics
  • SurveyMonkey: AI-assisted creation and analysis.
  • Typeform: conversational one-question-at-a-time UX; AI Form Builder generates from a prompt; "Clarify with AI" asks up to two follow-ups on vague open-ends.
  • Standard Insights: AI-driven analytics and survey platform.

Julius AI (the standout for data analysis): Upload CSV/Excel/Google Sheets and ask questions in plain English; Julius writes and runs Python or R code in a secure environment and returns charts, tables, and statistical output.

Using ChatGPT/Claude to write R or Python.

AI for cleaning, weighting, significance testing, crosstabs.

Section 5: Using AI for Reporting & Storytelling

Turning data into stories: The universal researcher complaint is that insight dies in the "data dump." AI helps at three points: structuring the narrative, drafting the executive summary, and building visuals.

The insight pyramid: the classic hierarchy — Data (what was collected) → Findings (what the data says) → Insights (what it means) → Recommendations (what to do).

AI is strong at the lower layers (summarizing data into findings) and can draft the upper layers, but the leap from finding to genuine insight is where human judgment earns its keep. Prompt AI to move up the pyramid explicitly: "For each finding below, state the insight (the 'so what') and one recommended action."

Tools:

  • Gamma: the strongest general AI presentation maker; text prompt → full deck in ~30 seconds.
  • Canva (Magic Studio): best template ecosystem and free tier;
  • PowerPoint Copilot

Executive summary from findings — example prompt: "You are a senior insights consultant. From the findings below, write a one-page executive summary in the McKinsey pyramid style: lead with the single most important answer, then three supporting insights, each with evidence and an implication. Tone: direct, decision-ready, no jargon."

Data visualization AI: Julius (auto-picks chart types from your data), Canva/Gamma for polished output, PowerPoint Copilot for Excel-linked charts.

Section 6: Using AI Safely & Securely — Confidentiality in Market Research

This is the section students most often ignore and most need.

The real risks:

  • Data leakage: anything pasted into a public consumer AI tool may be retained and, historically, used for training — effectively irretrievable.
  • Shadow AI: employees (and students) using unsanctioned personal AI accounts for work, outside any governance. Surveys show most workers use AI without disclosing it.
  • Training-data exposure: confidential inputs potentially surfacing in other users' outputs.

Tools with strong enterprise security: ChatGPT Enterprise, Claude Enterprise, and Microsoft Copilot all offer no-training-on-your-data guarantees and enterprise controls. Microsoft Copilot's advantage: it processes within your M365 tenant under existing governance - the safest option for regulated data.

Desktop/local AI models (the confidentiality power move): A local model runs entirely on your own machine — after a one-time download, nothing you type touches the internet. Ideal for sensitive transcripts and client data.

  • Ollama — command-line; ollama pull a model and run; runs as a local server other apps can connect to. Best for terminal-comfortable users.
  • LM Studio — ChatGPT-style GUI, strong model browser, OpenAI-compatible local server. Best all-in-one window; not open source.
  • GPT4All (Nomic) — lowest-friction entry point; "LocalDocs" lets the model answer from your own PDFs/files fully offline; runs on CPU-only hardware.
  • Jan.ai — privacy-first, 100% offline, no telemetry. All four run on the same llama.cpp engine and GGUF model format — so choose for interface, not speed. Practical minimum: 16GB RAM (8GB for small models).

Anonymizing inputs before public tools..

Student-specific confidentiality guidelines:

  • Treat every NDA as binding on your AI use - an NDA doesn't distinguish between telling a friend and pasting into ChatGPT.
  • Never upload client data, unpublished results, or participant recordings/transcripts to public tools.
  • Use university- or client-sanctioned enterprise tools, or a local model, for anything confidential.
  • When in doubt, anonymize; when still in doubt, don't paste.

Section 7: Synthetic Data

What it is: artificially generated data — created by AI/statistical models — that mimics real-world survey/panel responses. Kantar's definition: "based on surveying 1,000 real people... algorithms can create additional data that looks and behaves like it came from actual consumers." It is not new: data scientists have imputed predictive data for decades. What's new is generating data that purports to represent an individual's or group's attitudes.

How it's generated / categories (from IdSurvey/ESOMAR framing):

  1. Boosting/Augmenting/Imputation — topping up real samples to rebalance under-represented quotas.
  2. Fully synthetic — datasets built from scratch by models mimicking a population.
  3. Anonymized synthetic — real data transformed to protect privacy while keeping statistical properties. Methods include nearest-neighbour, Bayesian networks, and GANs.

Leading companies:

  • Kantar:Leads on synthetic sample boosting for brand health tracking; emphasizes starting from "an abundance of real baseline data." Its "digital twins" (persona bots) use past survey responses to predict reactions to new products.
  • Ipsos.
  • YouGov: Offers synthetic capability via its Yabble relationship.
  • MOSTLY AI: Synthetic data platform.
  • Gretel.

Digital twins: virtual models of a consumer/segment, continuously updated with data, used to predict future behavior and reactions — sometimes called "persona bots."

Bottom line on synthetic data: Good for early concept screening, scenario simulation, and boosting hard-to-reach demographics. Not a substitute for real human data in high-stakes decisions. Always validate against real feedback.

Recommendations

Stage 1 — Foundations:

  1. Master prompting: role-play, Chain-of-Thought, layered/iterative, and one framework (CLEAR). Build a personal prompt library.
  2. Learn the web-search-vs-training-data distinction cold. Default to Perplexity for facts, ChatGPT/Claude for synthesis.
  3. Internalize data hygiene before touching any real client/participant data: the Samsung rule. Set up a local model (GPT4All is the easiest) so you have a confidential option.

Stage 2 — Applied workflow: 4. Run one full secondary-research project through the 6-step workflow (Perplexity → Elicit/Consensus → Gigabrain → synthesize → fact-check). 5. Conduct one qual mini-project: draft a guide with AI, run/transcribe interviews, code and critically compare AI themes to your own. 6. Conduct one quant mini-project: build a survey in a free tier, analyze in Julius (student discount), and have it show you the R/Python code.

Stage 3 — Frontier & judgment: 7. Experiment with one AI-moderated platform and one synthetic-data tool — explicitly to learn their limits, not to trust them. 8. Read the primary industry sources.

Benchmarks that should change the approach:

  • If a tool won't show its sources or its code → downgrade trust, add manual verification.
  • If you're about to paste anything covered by an NDA → stop, anonymize or switch to a local/enterprise tool.
  • If synthetic data is informing a high-stakes go/no-go decision → require human validation before acting.
  • If AI themes/insights can't be traced back to a verbatim or a datapoint → treat them as hypotheses, not findings.
